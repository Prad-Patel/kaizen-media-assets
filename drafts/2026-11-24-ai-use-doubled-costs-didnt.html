The share of UK businesses using AI has doubled in a year, to 32%. But only 24% of the firms using it have seen their operating costs fall, and 64% have seen no change at all. That is the headline from the government's own AI adoption survey, published on 29 October, and it matches what we see when we sit down with owners: most businesses are chatting with AI, not putting it to work. Here is what the survey found, why the savings have not arrived yet, and the four moves we would make with a client to change that.

What the government survey found

The Department for Science, Innovation and Technology interviewed 3,503 UK businesses by phone between January and April 2026, then followed up with 50 in-depth interviews and seven site visits. Its executive summary sets out the main numbers:

  • Adoption has doubled. 32% of UK businesses were using at least one AI technology in 2026, up from 16% in 2025. Larger firms were further ahead, at 52%.
  • Most use is simple. 77% of those using or planning to use AI were using tools built on large language models, the ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini kind, mostly for writing and summarising text.
  • Admin and marketing lead. 81% used it in administration, 71% in marketing and 66% in IT.
  • Spending is light. 47% of those who could estimate it spent nothing on AI in 2025, which the report puts down to free tools and AI features already built into software they had.
  • Cost savings are rare. 24% reported lower operating costs, 64% saw no immediate financial effect and 9% saw costs go up.
  • It is not yet core. Only 14% said AI tools were of high or critical importance to their operations.

The good news is in there too. 71% of adopters reported better workforce productivity and 50% an overall increase in productivity, though the report describes those gains as generally modest. AI is helping people work a little faster. It is not yet taking whole jobs off anyone's plate.

Why most firms are not saving money yet

The survey's own wording is that AI is "functioning primarily as a support tool, augmenting existing activities rather than redefining business models". In plain terms, someone opens a chat window, asks for a draft email or a summary, copies the answer out and carries on. That saves a few minutes here and there, which is real, but it never shows up on the cost line because the job itself has not changed. A person still starts it, still moves the information between systems and still finishes it.

Three other findings explain why firms stall at that stage:

  • Skills, not price, are the barrier. Limited AI skills or knowledge affected 67% of adopters and 78% of firms planning to adopt. Where cost came up, it was mostly the time and effort to set things up, not the price of the tools.
  • No clear business case. 65% of non-adopters said they could not see a clear return on investment.
  • Trust. 56% of adopters had concerns about accuracy and bias, and 81% apply some human oversight to what AI produces.

None of that is a reason to stop. It is a reason to be more deliberate about which job you hand over, and how.

Four moves that put AI on the cost line

1. Pick one job that repeats, and time it

Start with a task that happens every day or every week, follows the same steps and eats time: turning enquiry emails into quotes, chasing unpaid invoices, logging receipts, answering the same five customer questions, booking appointments. Time it for a week before you change anything. Without a before figure you will never know whether AI saved you anything, which is exactly the "no clear business case" problem the survey found.

2. Move from asking to automating

The step most firms miss is connecting AI to where the work already lives. Instead of copying an enquiry into a chat window, an automation reads the email as it arrives, pulls out the details, drafts the quote in your template and drops it in your outbox for approval. Tools such as n8n, Zapier and Make do the connecting, and Claude, ChatGPT and Gemini now plug into Gmail, Outlook, calendars and cloud drives directly. This matches where firms say they are heading: in the survey, planned AI investment was focused on automating tasks (57%) and embedding AI into existing systems (54%). We built our own daily content pipeline this way, with AI doing the drafting and a person approving everything before it goes out.

3. Keep a human check where it matters

The survey found AI is trusted for low-risk routine work such as drafting and summarising, and much less for high-stakes decisions. That is the right instinct. Design the automation so a person approves anything that leaves the business, such as a quote, an invoice reminder or a reply to a complaint, and let it run on its own only for internal, low-risk steps. You keep the accuracy and still lose the copying and pasting.

4. Measure it after a month

Time the same job again four weeks in. If the hours have dropped, keep it and pick the next job. If they have not, find out why: often it is one step that still needs a person and can be redesigned. One automated job that saves an hour a day is worth more than ten chat tools that save a minute each.

What one automated job could be worth

An illustration, on our own assumptions rather than survey figures. Say turning enquiries into quotes takes someone 30 minutes a day. Over five days a week and 48 working weeks, that is 120 hours a year. Even at the National Living Wage of £12.71 an hour, that is about £1,525 of paid time a year on one job, before you count the enquiries that go cold while they wait. If an automation cuts that job to a five-minute check, most of those hours come back. Many automation tools have free or low-cost starter plans, so the bigger investment is usually the setup time, which is exactly what the survey found.

Where to start this week

  • List the five jobs you or your team repeat most often.
  • Time the most painful one for five working days.
  • Write down every step, and mark which ones are just moving information from one place to another. Those are the steps to automate.
  • Decide where a person must approve before anything goes to a customer.
  • Build or ask for one automation for that job, and time it again in a month.

The full AI adoption research report is worth a read if you want the sector detail. The short version is that using AI is no longer the edge, because a third of businesses already do. Putting it to work on a whole job, and measuring what it saves, is where the gap now opens up.
